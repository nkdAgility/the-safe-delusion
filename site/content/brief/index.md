Six organisations adopted the Scaled Agile Framework. This is what happened next, in the terms a board measures.

## ANZ Banking Group

{{< src who="ANZ Banking Group" note="Rolled out 2016, expanded 2017." anchor="anz-bank-initiatives-for-scaling-agile" >}}

Rolled out from 2016 and expanded across the Australian division from 2017, under a mandate to deliver faster and underpin the bank's mortgage business.

Five years on, its home loan approvals were among the slowest in Australia. The bank lost substantial share of a $2 trillion mortgage market. Its share price was down 17% across the chief executive's seven-year tenure.

An industry analyst's assessment was that this is not an agile organisation, and *"quite sluggish in terms of some of the things that they're doing."*

## Capital One

{{< src who="Capital One" note="Vendor case study published 2017." anchor="capital-one" >}}

The programme set out to move beyond business-and-IT alignment, with the entire workforce trained and key roles certified.

In January 2023 the company eliminated all 1,100 of its Agile roles. The announcement made clear the practice had stayed siloed inside IT, without the business involvement the programme existed to achieve.

{{% contradiction %}}
Scaled Agile published Capital One as a customer success story in 2017. The case study remained published after the roles were cut.
{{% /contradiction %}}

## Peaksys

{{< src who="Peaksys" note="Adopted c. 2020, later stopped." anchor="peaksys" >}}

Adopted around 2020 to support entry into a new market.

Time from prioritising a feature to activating it for customers ran to approximately **one year**. Work in progress and multitasking rose. A commitment cycle that freezes priorities for a quarter could not accommodate a fast-moving product bet.

They concluded the framework was holding them back, and stopped.

## Volvo Cars

{{< src who="Volvo Cars" note="Two-and-a-half-year transformation, 2017–2019." anchor="volvo-cars" >}}

Two and a half years of transformation across an organisation employing around 10,000 people in software.

By September 2022 a department of eleven teams and just over a hundred people had dropped it. Their assessment: the quarterly planning event had become largely presentational, and the coordination artefact it produced added very little against the effort it consumed. The real work was organised elsewhere.

## Fitbit

{{< src who="Fitbit" note="Adopted 2015–2016, later abandoned." anchor="fitbit" >}}

The success claimed in the vendor's case study rests on company growth and product performance. That account is contradicted by the company's public financial data.

Fitbit later abandoned the framework, and the person who had championed it left.

{{% contradiction %}}
Fitbit remained listed on the vendor's website as a success story after the framework had been abandoned.
{{% /contradiction %}}

## United States Air Force

{{< src who="U.S. Air Force" note="Chief Software Officer memorandum, December 2019." anchor="us-air-force" >}}

In December 2019 the Chief Software Officer issued a memorandum strongly discouraging rigid, prescriptive frameworks, naming SAFe.

Scaled Agile publicly offered free consulting to address the concerns. The USAF subsequently confirmed the memorandum's conclusions stood, and that the framework would not be used in any form in its DevSecOps programme.

## What the evidence adds up to

{{< src who="General conclusions" anchor="general-conclusions" >}}

Across every case that permits independent verification, the guide finds **no evidence of lasting benefit** against the time and money spent — and finds that the weaknesses of the operating models these organisations were trying to leave behind were amplified rather than reduced.

The organisations that recovered did so after stopping. Beijer Electronics left in June 2021 and improvement followed. Peaksys stopped. The Volvo department dropped it and worked more fluidly without it.

## Before you commit further

{{% editor %}}
Three questions worth putting to whoever is accountable for the programme:

1. **How long does it currently take us to get one decision in front of a customer?** Peaksys measured a year and treated that as the finding.
2. **Are we removing dependencies between teams, or scheduling around them?** The two produce very different cost curves, and only one compounds in your favour.
3. **What would we expect to see if this were not working?** If the answer is *"we would need more time and more training"*, that condition cannot be failed.
{{% /editor %}}
